About this exercise

This is a learning exercise only. The seed phrase you make here is not for holding (self-custody of) real bitcoin. This page runs in a web browser on a computer that is connected to the internet, and anything typed on an online computer could be seen by malware or other software.

Real keys can be made completely offline: on a computer that has never been connected to the internet, or entirely by hand with dice or coins, pencil, paper and the same math you'll do here.

The point of this exercise is to walk you through, step by step, what a hardware wallet or wallet software does inside when it creates your keys. A wallet does all of this in a fraction of a second. Here you do it slowly enough to see every step: randomness → bits → checksum → numbers → words → seed.

What this exercise covers: the whole path a wallet follows. BIP-39 takes you from randomness to your 24 words and the 512-bit seed. Then BIP-32 turns that seed into a master key, walks down a derivation path one level at a time, and ends at a real Bitcoin address made from your own dice or coin flips.

⚠ Read this first

  • Never share your 24 words. Anyone who has your words owns the bitcoin, with no way to get it back. (If the wallet also uses a passphrase, the words alone aren't enough, but never count on a passphrase to protect leaked words.) No real company, wallet, exchange or support person will ever ask for them.
  • Use real dice or a real coin. Don't make up your bits. People are bad at being random. Bits you pick in your head, patterns (like 0101…), or all 0s or all 1s make words that thieves can guess. Thieves run programs that constantly check weak, guessable keys and empty any bitcoin they find.

The big picture

This is everything a wallet does to make a seed, and it fills in with your numbers as you work. Each box is one step below. The highlighted box is the one you're on.

Only the first box has any randomness in it. Every step after that is fixed math: the same input always gives the same output. That's why your words can rebuild your wallet anywhere, and why weak randomness in box 1 can never be fixed later.

Step 1Roll dice or flip a coin, and type your bits

What's happening: every Bitcoin wallet starts as one big random number. (All of the wallet's keys are later worked out from it, step by step, with no more randomness added.) A wallet gets its randomness from a hardware random number generator inside its chip; you'll get yours from dice or a coin, which you can see. Each bit is a coin-flip choice (0 or 1), and 256 of them give 2256 possible keys, a 78-digit number, close to the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe. That's why nobody can guess a truly random key, and why made-up or patterned bits are dangerous.

Use whichever you have. Each roll or flip gives you one bit.

  • Dice: roll several at once and read them left to right: 1, 2 or 3 = 0  ·  4, 5 or 6 = 1.
  • Coin: flip it and let it land: tails = 0  ·  heads = 1. Flip 256 times. Don't catch it, and don't decide what to call it after you see it.

Type one bit per yellow box: 11 per row for rows 1–23, then 3 in row 24. Each row is split 4 – 4 – 3 to help you keep your place. The orange boxes are the checksum. You'll get them in Step 2.

Tip: the cursor jumps to the next box by itself. You can also paste a string of 0s and 1s into any box.

Step 2Find the checksum

What's happening: your 256 bits get run through SHA-256, a "hash" function. It always gives the same 64-character answer for the same input, but change even one bit and the answer changes completely, and there's no way to work backward from the answer to the input. The BIP-39 standard takes the first 8 bits of that hash (the first two hex characters) and adds them to the end of your bits. That's the checksum: if someone later writes down a wrong word, the checksum won't match and the wallet will warn them. 256 + 8 = 264 bits, which divides evenly into 24 groups of 11.

Step 3Work out your words

What's happening: 11 bits can make 211 = 2048 different numbers, from 0 to 2047, and the BIP-39 word list has exactly 2048 words. So each group of 11 bits picks one word. Reading a group of bits as a number works like reading a regular number, only each place is worth double the one to its right instead of ten times: 1, 2, 4, 8 … 1024. The words don't add anything new. They're just an easier way for people to write down and read back the same 264 bits without mistakes.

Your job, for each row: turn the row's 11 bits into a number, then find its word. Three steps:

  1. Find M. Every column has a number at the top: 1024, 512, 256, 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Go across the row. Wherever the bit is a 1, write down the number at the top of that column. Wherever it is a 0, skip it. Add up what you wrote down. That total is M. Type it in the "Your M" box.
  2. Find N. Add 1 to M. The numbers start at 0, but the word list starts at line 1, so N = M + 1.
  3. Find the word. Look up line N in the word list and type that word in the "Your word" box.

Step 4This is your actual seed

🔒 Locked. This step opens when your checksum and all 24 words are right.

Your 24 words are not the seed. They're a human-friendly way of writing down 264 bits: your 256 random bits from the dice or coin, plus the 8-bit checksum.

What's a passphrase? Your 24 words are all a wallet needs. But BIP-39 also lets you add an optional passphrase: any extra text you choose, sometimes called the “25th word.” It's an extra secret. Someone who finds your 24 words still can't open the wallet without it. But lose the passphrase, and your words alone won't get the bitcoin back. A passphrase adds only as much security as it is hard to guess, so it never makes up for weak dice or coin bits. Most wallets leave it blank unless you turn it on.

To build the real seed, your wallet writes your 24 words as one sentence (a single space between words) and feeds that text, not the bits, into a function called PBKDF2 as its password. The passphrase goes in as the salt: extra text that gets mixed in with the password before hashing, so the same password with a different salt gives a completely different result. In BIP-39, the salt is the word mnemonic with your passphrase added on the end, so no passphrase means the salt is just mnemonic. PBKDF2 runs HMAC-SHA512 2,048 times in a chain, each round feeding the next, and combines all 2,048 results (it XORs them together) into one answer. The result is the 512-bit number below, the BIP-39 seed.

That stretching is deliberate: it makes each guess 2,048 times slower for an attacker. But stretching is not randomness. It can't rescue weak or made-up bits. A patterned or guessable phrase is still found quickly, just a little more slowly. Your security comes from your 256 real dice or coin bits.

Working…

Each round's output (U) is completely different from the one before. The seed is all 2,048 of them combined with XOR, so an attacker can't skip ahead: to test one guess, they have to run all 2,048 rounds too.

Your BIP-39 seed (128 hex characters = 512 bits):

Every private key, public key and address in your wallet comes from this one number. The wallet hashes it once more (HMAC-SHA512 with the key "Bitcoin seed", the BIP-32 standard) to make the master private key, and every address is built from that. You'll do exactly that in Steps 6 and 7.

Change a single word, or add a single letter of passphrase, and you get a completely different seed and a completely different wallet. Try it:

What does the strength meter mean?

A passphrase only matters if someone has already got your 24 words. Then it's the only lock left, and they can try guesses as fast as their computers allow. BIP-39 stretches each guess with just 2,048 rounds of PBKDF2, so guesses are fairly cheap. The meter shows roughly how long three kinds of attacker would need on average: an ordinary attacker with one regular computer, a specialized attacker with a dedicated rig of graphics cards, and a nation state with an enormous budget. The speeds are rough orders of magnitude, not exact figures. Real attacks on passphrases are rare, because the attacker first has to get your 24 words. A time shown in red is under a year.

It's a rough estimate. It counts length and character types, and gives less credit for common passwords, keyboard runs, repeats, and BIP-39 words. A passphrase built from randomly chosen words (rolled with dice, not picked by you) is strong and easy to remember: each random word from the 2,048 list adds 11 bits.

Forget the passphrase and the bitcoin is gone. There's no reset. A wrong passphrase doesn't give an error, it opens a different, empty wallet. Back it up as carefully as the words, but store it separately.

This is the one step you can't check by hand, so verify it by comparing it against an independent tool like Ian Coleman's, which you can find at github.com/iancoleman/bip39. Paste your 24 words (and the same passphrase) into its "BIP39 Mnemonic" box, and its "BIP39 Seed" field should match yours exactly.

Step 6From seed to master key (BIP-32)

🔒 Locked. This step opens once your seed is showing in Step 4.
What's happening: one more hash. The wallet runs your seed through HMAC-SHA512 again, this time with the words Bitcoin seed as the key. The answer is 512 bits, shown below as 128 hex characters.

The wallet doesn't use those 512 bits as one number. It cuts them exactly in half:

  • First half (256 bits) → master private key. The key at the very top of your wallet. Every other key comes from it.
  • Second half (256 bits) → chain code. An extra secret. The wallet mixes it in every time it makes a key below this one (Step 7 shows why that matters).

Step 7Down the derivation path to your first address

🔒 Locked. This step opens when you've split your master key in Step 6.
What's happening: from the master key, the wallet derives a child: HMAC-SHA512 again, keyed with the parent's chain code. The left half of the result is added to the parent private key to make the child key; the right half is the child's chain code. Repeat that from child to child and you walk down a path. A wallet's address keys live five levels down, at a path like m/84'/0'/0'/0/0.
LevelIn m/84'/0'/0'/0/0What it means
Purpose84'Which standard, and so which address type: 44' legacy, 49' nested SegWit, 84' native SegWit, 86' Taproot
Coin type0'0' is bitcoin, 1' is testnet
Account0'Separate accounts from the same seed, like separate bank accounts
Change00 is for addresses you hand out to receive; 1 is for change back to yourself
Index0Address number 0, 1, 2… Wallets look ahead about 20 unused addresses (the "gap limit") when restoring

The apostrophe means hardened. A hardened child uses an index from 231 (2,147,483,648) upward, so 84' really means index 231 + 84, and its HMAC input includes the parent's private key. A normal child (no apostrophe) uses the parent's public key instead. That one difference decides who can derive what, as you'll see at the end.

Why the first three levels are hardened: the xpub leak

Your account xpub (above) can be given to a watch-only wallet or an accounting tool: it can make all your addresses but can't spend. This demo asks a what if: suppose someone got that xpub and the private key for one address below it. Watch what they could do with nothing but public math.

Before you run it: this is not a crack in Bitcoin. Nothing here breaks SHA-256, HMAC or the elliptic curve. It's a known trade-off in how wallets derive keys, written into the BIP-32 standard itself, and wallets were designed around it from the start. The math is easy only if the attacker already holds two things at once:
  1. Your xpub. You only share it on purpose, with a tool you chose.
  2. A private key for one of your addresses. Normal wallets never show or export these. Hardware wallets can't export them at all: the key never leaves the device.
Without both, there's no shortcut. Guessing a private key means searching about 2128 possibilities, far beyond every computer on Earth running for the age of the universe.
